Behaviour (optional)

A very large number, or blank, means it appeared to hold still. Orbital motion has both a ceiling and a floor at any given altitude, so this tests in both directions.
WHAT THIS TOOL DOES AND DOES NOT DO It tests one hypothesis: could sunlight have reached an object in orbit along the reported line of sight at the reported time? The answer depends on the bearing as much as the time — low in the sunward sky, low Earth orbit stays lit for hours after the rest of the sky is dark, so never restate a result as a “sun was too far down” threshold without the bearing attached. A negative result is strong — it eliminates satellites and debris on physical grounds. A positive result eliminates nothing; it only means the hypothesis survives, and you still need a pass prediction, a motion check, and a brightness check. This tool never identifies an object.

The shadow ceiling has two branches. Above the Sun’s depression angle the line of sight leaves the shadow on the near side; below it, the sightline crosses the shadow axis and exits the far side at a much greater distance, never closer than R·(csc β/2 − 1). Directions below the depression angle are therefore demanding, not impossible — the tool solves both branches and reports which one applies.

Model: geometric cylindrical shadow, Earth radius 6371 km, opaque atmosphere allowance 80 km, solar position by the NOAA algorithm (no refraction correction, so β is accurate to about half a degree within the first few minutes after sunset and better thereafter). Brightness figures are order-of-magnitude estimates and depend on object size, shape, coating, and phase angle — treat them as guidance, not measurement.

Verify every surviving hypothesis against real orbital elements: heavens-above.com, celestrak.org, n2yo.com. Use elements dated within a few days of the sighting.
